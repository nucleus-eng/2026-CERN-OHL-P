# We take timeseries and you should too!
Fluorescence is roughly proportional to reporter concentration, which makes steady-state fluorescence a simple and effective readout of total expression capacity. This makes endpoint measurements attractive, but they have some drawbacks. First, different reactions can have the same endpoint fluorescence and run at different rates. Second, different labs may incubate their samples for different times, thus the "end" of a reaction in one lab may not be the same as in another. Third, fluorescence at the end of the *experiment* may not be the same as at the end of the *reaction* if your samples show any "drift" after reaching steady state (more on that later). Fourth, most of the work and cost of running a reaction comes in assembling it, so taking only one measurement throws out free data. **Timeseries measurements** can answer questions that endpoints can't at the cost of more post-processing. We think that trade is worth it and built analysis software into the CDK to make it easier for ourselves and others.

Fluorescence-versus-time plots helped us find some strange behaviors that were otherwise hidden in simple barplots of endpoints. For instance:

- Reactions that reach steady state, and then slowly **drift**: this effect appears affect individual wells independent of their composition. We don't know why this happens -- maybe slow evaporation from a poor seal? -- but ignoring drift can inflate or deflate your estimates of protein expression.
- Reactions that reach the **same steady state at different rates**: conditions looks identical at steady state, but represent genuinely different underlying kinetics.
- **Biphasic traces**: some conditions have given us an initial fast expression phase followed by a slower second one. This reflects some interesting mechanism (perhaps rNTP depletion and re-phosphorylation?) that we have yet to describe.

Not only do these kind of observations motivate mechanistic modeling, but theyy are invisible in an endpoint assay. Moreover, the richer data generated in timeseries experiments can better inform those models. As the Nucleus Community grows and more groups share cytosol data, information-dense timeseries will compound in value. We can always estimate the endpoint from a timeseries, but we can't go the other way. We strongly encourage the Nucleus community to collect timeseries now, even if we aren't all yet ready to use them, so that the data is there when the tools catch up.

# How we model cytosol kinetics

A timeseries is an information rich object: every trace is shaped by many real, biological factors -- e.g., transcription, translation initiation, fluorophore maturation, resource depletion, degradation — mixed with sources of noise and bias -- e.g., differences in liquid handling, evaporation, photobleaching. 
The goal of fitting is *not* to replace that richness, but rather to *summarize* key aspects of the curve in a compact representation that we can think and talk about. We also want diagnostic metrics to help us decide when our simple summary is missing details and that we need a richer representation. In short, we want "portable" metrics that you can put in a table, compare against input variables, talk about with a collaborator, hand over to a modeler, or publish in a DevNote.

Cytosol reactions show four (4) typical phases: 

1. **Lag:** the system begins transcription, translation, and reporter maturation. No mature reporter yet exists so signal is flat or nearly so.
2. **Rise:** reporter begins to accumulate and the resulting fluorescence follows a sigmoid curve.
3. **Steady State:** protein expression slows down and stops (typically < 4 hr) and fluorescence intensity levels out. Fluorescence at this point is a good metric for the total amount of reporter expressed over the course of the reaction.
4. **Drift:** signal (may) shift over longer periods of time (e.g., during overnight incubation) due to effects other than transcription and translation (e.g., evaporation or photobleaching).

These reactions are well approximated by a logistic curve with a linear drift term,

$$
F(t) = F_{ss}\frac{1}{1 + e^{-k(t-\tau_{vel})}} + d(t- \tau_{drift}), 
$$

with the following parameters:
| Fit Parameter | Name | Units | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
|$F_{ss}$ | Steady State Fluorescence | $u$ | Fluorescence at the endpoint of the reaction. Proportional to total amount of reporter expressed. |
| $k$ | Steepness | unitless | How fast is the reaction. |
| $\tau_{vel}$  | Inflection Time | $h$ | How long until the reaction reaches its fastest rate. |
| $d$ | Drift Slope | $u \cdot h^{-1}$| The rate of drift after steady state (ideally, close to zero). |
| $\tau_{drift}$| Drift Onset | $h$ | When does drift start to affect fluorescence. |

In addition to the above fit parameters, we also report three derived parameters: 

- **Maximum reaction rate ($V_{max}$):**  the maximum slope of the sigmoid, which occurs at $\tau_{vel}$ and equals $\frac{F_{ss} \cdot k}{4}$. This is *not* the Michaelis–Menten $V_{max}$. When comparing across conditions, prefer reporting `k` directly. Only use $\frac{F_{ss} \cdot k}{4}$ for downstream calculations that require a slope with units ($u \cdot h^{-1}$).
- **Lag time ($\tau_{lag}$):** the time it takes for the reaction to "start" (units of $h$). Explicitly, the x-intercept of the line tangent to the inflection point ($\tau_{vel}$) on the sigmoid. This is metric is related to transcription lag time, translation lag time, and the maturation time of GFP. 
- **Time-to-steady-state ($\tau_{ss}$):** the time at which the reaction is "finished" (units of $h$). Explicitly, when the sigmoid reaches a chosen fraction $\alpha$ of its asymptote (default $\alpha=0.95$). $\tau_{ss}$ can be derived analytically from the model as
  $$
  \tau_{ss} \;=\; t_0 + \frac{1}{k}\ln\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right).
  $$
Drift is intentionally excluded from this calculation as we believe its exclusion gives us more accurate estimates of when the reaction finished.


# How to use the CDK to model your reactions
This primer assumes you already have:

- data from an experiment in a format that the CDK can handle (see [`cdk.instrument.platereader`](https://github.com/bnext-bio/bnext/tree/main/cdk/src/cdk/analysis/cytosol) for details),
- a platemap that describes what's in each well of your experiment (see the *Why you should use a platemap* DevNote),
- (optional) a fluorescent standard on the plate that you can use to normalize your data (see the *Why you should use standards* DevNote).

You can use the `platereader` module of the CDK to load platreader data and plot kinetic fits. Here's an code snippet example:
```python
from cdk.analysis.cytosol import platereader as pr

data = pr.load_platereader_data(
    path="path-to-timecourse-data.txt",
    platemap="path-to-platemap.csv",
)

graph, kinetics_df = pr.plot_kinetics(
    data, 
    fit_function_name="sigmoid_drift"
)

```
This code snippet produces the following per group of conditions:
- raw traces with an overlay of the model fit
- a summary plot of $F_{ss}$, $V_{max}$, $\tau_{lag}$, and $\tau_{ss}$
- a tidy DataFrame of the fit parameters


:::{tip}
The CDK currently defaults to `fit_function_name="sigmoid_drift"`. If you don't want to model drift, replace `"sigmoid_drift"` with `"sigmoid"`.
:::

# Common pitfalls and limitations

**Truncated reactions.** If the reaction hasn't actually completed by the last time-point, the CDK will cheerfully return a poor fit with small residuals. This is a known bug and we are working on building automated checks for this. CHECK YOUR FITS by visually inspecting  if the curve reaches steady state and how well the fit matches the curve. 

**Reporting results without normalization.** Before normalization, your data is in your plate reader's native fluorescence units (arbitrary). For comparisons within the same experimental run, this is acceptable, however these data will not be comparable to other experiments. You can normalize your fluorescences to a known, calibrated fluorescent standard. See [fluorescence standards](url-to-standards devnot) devnote for details.

**Sigmoid doesn't describe your data**. The sigmoid fit is good at summarizing a simple, "well-behaved" trace in four numbers, but has drawbacks. Specifically, it cannot represent:
- asymmetric rise/fall (use Gompertz or a generalized sigmoid),
- a biphasic reaction, e.g., when a module starts working later in a run (ClpXP-mediated degradation catching up to production),
- explicit resource-exhaustion dynamics (better captured by a mechanistic model).
